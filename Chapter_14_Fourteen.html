Chapter Eight, Part One. Powering Civilization, Energy Transformation.


Energy is the foundation of civilization. Every economic activity, every technological advance, every aspect of modern life depends on the conversion of energy from one form to another. The coming decade will see a transformation of global energy systems that rivals the shift from wood to coal or coal to oil in previous centuries. This transformation is driven by three forces: climate imperatives requiring decarbonization, economic advantages of renewable energy reaching cost parity and beyond, and the massive energy demands of artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure. The result will be a global energy system that is cleaner, more distributed, more resilient, and vastly larger than today's. The Renewable Revolution Renewable energy has crossed critical thresholds: Solar Power: Global solar capacity exceeding 2.5 terawatts as of 2026, having crossed the 2 terawatt milestone in late 2024 Costs declined 90 percent over the past decade, making solar the cheapest electricity source in most locations Utility-scale projects under 0.02 dollars/kWh in optimal locations Distributed rooftop solar democratizing energy production Perovskite and tandem cell technologies promising further efficiency gains. Wind Power: Offshore wind expanding rapidly with turbines exceeding 15MW capacity Floating wind accessing deeper waters with stronger, steadier winds Onshore wind remains highly cost-competitive Grid integration challenges driving storage and demand response. Bloomberg NEF New EnergyOutlook 2026: Their analysis projects that solar will become the largest source of electricity globally by 2035, but notes that A.I. data centers will keep fossil fuels in business longer than hoped-data center energy demand is growing faster than renewable additions in some regions. Record Year for Renewables: Solar and wind are both projected to surpass nuclear power output globally for the first time in 2026, backed by record battery storage deployment. Storage and Grid Transformation The critical enabler: Battery Storage: Lithium-ion battery costs declined 90 percent over the past decade Grid-scale installations growing exponentially Utility-scale batteries providing 4-8 hours of storage Longer-duration storage technologies emerging (flow batteries, compressed air, gravity). Smart Grids: Digital management of distributed energy resources Bidirectional power flows (consumers as producers) Demand response managing consumption to match generation Microgrids improving resilience Nuclear Energy: A Renaissance Nuclear power sees renewed interest: Advanced Reactors: Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) from Nu Scale, Rolls-Royce, and others High-temperature gas reactors for industrial process heat Molten salt reactors with passive safety features Fusion research advancing (see below). Existing Fleet: Plant life extensions keeping reactors operating longer Restart of previously closed plants in some regions Uranium supply chain challenges emerging The A.I. Energy Challenge A new and significant demand driver: Data centers consumed 1 to 2 percent of global electricity in 2022 A.I. training and inference could increase this to 4 to 8 percent by 2030 Single large A.I. training runs consuming as much energy as small cities Tech companies signing massive renewable energy contracts Nuclear energy being reconsidered specifically for A.I. power Shell's 2026 Energy Security Scenarios explore how nations can navigate geopolitical tensions while powering an A.I. revolution, emphasizing the tension between energy security and decarbonization. JPMorgan 2026 Energy Outlook focuses on mitigating energy volatility in this transitioning landscape. Fossil Fuels: The Declining Base Oil, gas, and coal remain significant but declining: Peak oil demand: Projected by most analysts in the late 2020s or early 2030s. Natural gas: Bridge fuel facing pressure but remaining important for reliability. Coal: Rapidly declining in developed nations, growing in some developing economies. Stranded assets: Trillions in fossil fuel reserves that may never be extracted The Promise Fusion-the process powering the sun-offers virtually unlimited clean energy: Fuel (deuterium from water, lithium) essentially inexhaustible No long-lived radioactive waste Inherent safety (reaction stops if confinement fails) No greenhouse gas emissions Current Progress (2026) ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor): The largest fusion project, under construction in France First plasma now expected around 2033 to 2034 after project delays, with deuterium-tritium reactions targeted for the late 2030s Demonstrating feasibility of sustained fusion reactions. Private Fusion Companies: Commonwealth Fusion Systems (MIT spinout): Demonstrating high-temperature superconducting magnets TAE Technologies: Advanced beam-driven fusion approach Helion Energy: Plans commercial fusion by 2028 (ambitious timeline) Zap Energy: Z-pinch approach with compact design. Government Research: Laser fusion (National Ignition Facility) achieved scientific breakeven Magnetic confinement advances improving plasma performance Alternative approaches (stellarators, field-reversed configuration) progressing. Recent Analysis: A March 2026 Nature Energy paper ("Fusion power experience rates are overestimated") suggests that historical learning rates for fusion may be overly optimistic, implying the technology may take longer to commercialize than enthusiasts hope. The Timeline Challenge Fusion has been "30 years away" for 70 years. Realistic projections: Demonstration plant: 2035 to 2045 (net energy gain sustained). First commercial plant: 2045 to 2055. Meaningful grid contribution: 2050 plus Dominant energy source: 2060 plus if successful Fusion is not a solution for the 2026 to 2036 decade, but research investment now determines whether it becomes available for the decades beyond. Phase 1: Renewable Dominance (2026 to 2028) 2026 to 2027: Cost and deployment milestones: Solar and wind providing 40 percent plus of electricity in leading countries Battery storage costs declining another 50 percent Green hydrogen production scaling in regions with cheap renewables Coal phase-out accelerating in developed nations 2027 to 2028: Grid transformation: Smart grid technology standard in new installations Vehicle-to-grid integration (E.V.s as distributed storage) Dynamic pricing managing demand to match renewable supply First 100 percent renewable days in major grids Phase 2: Systemic Transition (2028 to 2032) 2028 to 2030: Electrification of transport and heat: Electric vehicles reaching 50 percent plus of new car sales in leading markets Heat pumps replacing gas heating in many applications Industrial electrification (steel, cement, chemicals) Green hydrogen for steel, ammonia, and shipping fuel 2030 to 2032: Energy system transformation: Solar the largest global electricity source Fossil fuel demand peaking and declining Massive grid investment for renewable integration Nuclear SMRs entering commercial operation Phase 3: Decarbonization and Abundance (2032 to 2036) 2032 to 2036: The clean energy system: Developed nations approaching 80 percent plus clean electricity Global CO2 emissions declining significantly Energy abundance rather than scarcity in sunny/windy regions Advanced nuclear and long-duration storage maturing Fusion research approaching demonstration phase Electricity Generation The core transformation: Solar: Dominant new capacity globally. Wind: Major contributor, especially offshore. Nuclear: Stable baseload where accepted. Hydro: Existing asset, limited expansion. Gas: Peaking and backup role. Coal: Rapid phase-out where economically and politically feasible Transportation Fueling mobility: Light-duty vehicles: Rapid electrification. Heavy-duty trucks: Battery and hydrogen fuel cell competition. Shipping: Ammonia and methanol as marine fuels. Aviation: Sustainable aviation fuels (SAF) and eventual hydrogen. Rail: Already largely electrified, completing the transition Industry Powering production: Steel: Hydrogen-based direct reduction replacing blast furnaces. Cement: Process heat electrification and carbon capture. Chemicals: Green hydrogen feedstock replacing natural gas. Aluminum: Already electricity-intensive, decarbonizing grid. Data centers: Massive efficiency improvements and renewable matching Buildings Heating and cooling: Heat pumps: Replacing gas furnaces and boilers. Efficiency: Building codes requiring net-zero or near-net-zero. Demand response: Smart systems managing consumption.
