Lessons: Predictions about specific dates often wrong Predictions about direction often right Experts systematically overestimate short-term and underestimate long-term The most reliable predictions are about trends, not dates Many technologies exhibit exponential improvement: Moore's. Law: Transistor density doubles every 2 years 1971: 2,300 transistors (Intel 4004) 2023: 100 plus billion (Apple M3) 50 years: 50 million x improvement KURTS-vile's. Law of Accelerating Returns: Evolution of technology itself accelerates Each technology generation enables the next Feedback loops create super-exponential growth The "knee of the curve": What looks gradual becomes sudden Wright's. Law: Cost declines predictably with cumulative production Solar: 20 percent cost reduction per doubling of capacity Batteries: 15 to 20 percent cost reduction per doubling Applies to many manufacturing technologies Technologies typically follow S-curves: 1. Slow initial phase: Early development, limited adoption 2. Rapid growth phase: Technology proven, adoption accelerates 3. Maturity phase: Market saturation, incremental improvements. Implications: Predicting which phase a technology is in is critical A.I. may be near the inflection point where slow initial progress becomes rapid growth. Value increases with number of users: Metcalfe's. Law: Value proportional to n^2 (connections) Reed's. Law: Value proportional to 2^n (subgroups). Platform economics: Winner-take-most dynamics A.I.: More users ? more data ? better A.I. ? more users In technology, rare events matter disproportionately: Safety: 0.1 percent edge cases cause 99 percent of accidents. Business: 1 percent of customers often generate 50 percent plus of revenue. Innovation: 1 percent of ideas create 99 percent of value. The gap: We spend orders of magnitude less on preventing civilization-threatening risks than we spend on managing much smaller risks (airline safety, automotive safety, etc.). The numbers tell a clear story: transformative technologies are advancing rapidly, investment is massive, and the stakes are existential. The 2026 to 2036 decade will see more technological change than any comparable period in history. The data also reveals gaps: between capability and governance, between investment in development and investment in safety, between national interests and global cooperation. Addressing these gaps is the defining challenge of our era.
